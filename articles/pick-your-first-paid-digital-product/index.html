5 Steps to Pick Your First Paid Digital Product (Without Starting From Zero)

Start from what you already have, write one buyer, and try to kill the idea before you build it

DigitalShelf Labs · 16 October 2026

Plenty of people who build things (scripts, templates, small automations, setup notes) still have no paid listing. Usually it's not a lack of ideas. It's that choosing one and putting a price on it feels like a bigger decision than it is. These five steps make it small.

1. Start with what you already have

Don't invent something new. Your first product should come from something you've already built, written or fixed more than once. Spend thirty minutes writing four quick lists:

  1. Tools, scripts, templates or setups you built for yourself.
  2. Problems you've solved at least twice, for yourself or someone else.
  3. Questions people ask you more than once.
  4. Notes, docs or checklists you keep for your own use.

Circle the one item where you'd be least embarrassed if a stranger paid for a cleaned-up version. You're not committing, just picking something to test.

2. Write one buyer in one sentence

Not "founders" or "creators." One kind of person, at one moment, with one problem:

A [specific who] who is [in this situation] and is worried about [the thing that could go wrong].

Check it: could you find ten of these people online this week? Would they recognise themselves? Is it a moment rather than a personality? "About to launch" beats "ambitious."

3. Pick one offer with a checkable outcome

Decide what you're selling to that one person, and keep it small enough to finish in a day. A checklist, a template, a short guide or a config file with instructions all count.

Two rules. One format, not a checklist plus a video plus a community. And one outcome the buyer can check: "switched to live payments without broken webhooks" is checkable; "feels more confident" isn't. Write it as: [Format] that helps [buyer] [checkable outcome] [in what time or setting].

4. Try to kill it before you build it

It's much cheaper to drop an idea now than after you've written the listing. Answer yes or no:

  1. Is it already free, complete and easy to find in roughly the same form?
  2. Would you need to be a credentialed expert to sell it safely (legal, medical, tax, financial)?
  3. Would it go out of date within three months in a way you won't keep up with?
  4. Can you not name a single place your buyer hangs out online?
  5. Would you be uncomfortable putting your name on it?
  6. Does it need calls or setup help from you to be useful?
  7. Could you not finish a first version tomorrow?

Two or more yeses: drop it and go back to your second choice. One note on the first question: free information existing isn't a reason to stop. People pay for something sorted, short and in the right order. It's only a yes if the free version already is that.

5. Set one price by comparison

No tiers, no launch discount. You're testing whether anyone pays at all, and one price makes that easy to read.

Find three to five comparable paid products for a similar buyer and of a similar size. Note each one's price and what's included. If yours is smaller or narrower, price toward the low end. If it prevents a specific, costly mistake, price toward the middle. Pick a plain round number and write one sentence on why. You can change a price in a minute once you see real behaviour; you learn nothing from a listing that never goes up.
