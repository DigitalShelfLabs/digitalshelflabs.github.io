The 30-Minute Monthly Routine That Keeps a Rental Property Spreadsheet Accurate

What to track, what to check each month, and seven mistakes that quietly break landlord spreadsheets

DigitalShelf Labs · 17 October 2026

A rental property spreadsheet is easy to start and easy to abandon. The first month it's tidy. By month four, two payments are missing, a repair receipt is in a jacket pocket, and nobody is sure whether the deposit for the upstairs flat was ever recorded.

The spreadsheet isn't usually the problem. The routine is. Here's a simple one for landlords with a handful of long-term rentals: what to record, a 30-minute check once a month, a short year-end review, and the mistakes worth avoiding.

This is about record-keeping, not legal, tax or accounting advice. Rules on deposits, notice periods, late fees and taxes differ by country and region, so check your local rules or ask a qualified professional.

What to record (and nothing more)

You don't need to track everything. You need to track the things you'll be asked about later, by a tenant, an accountant or yourself.

  • Units: an ID for each unit (U01, U02…), the property it belongs to and whether it's let or vacant.
  • Leases: tenant, start and end dates, monthly rent, the day rent is due, deposit amount and notice period.
  • Rent: one row for each rent period due, with the amount due, date paid and amount paid. If someone pays in two parts, record both.
  • Deposits: amount held, where it's held, any deductions (with the reason) and refunds.
  • Expenses: date, unit (or "shared" for costs across the whole portfolio), category, payee, amount, and where the receipt is saved.
  • Repairs and maintenance: what broke, who's fixing it, cost, status and a follow-up date. Recurring jobs, like a heating service or alarm test, get a "last done" date and how often they repeat.

If a piece of information doesn't help you answer "who owes what", "what did this unit earn" or "what's due next", leave it out. Fewer columns means fewer gaps.

The monthly close: about 30 minutes

Pick a fixed day, for example the 5th, after most rent has arrived. Then work through the same five steps every time.

1. Match rent to your bank statement (10 minutes). Open the statement for last month and tick off every rent payment against the sheet. Anything in the bank but not in the sheet gets added. Anything expected but not paid stays as a balance. Don't round, and don't net payments together.

2. Look at who still owes (5 minutes). Sort or filter by balance. Note partial payments and how many days late each one is. Decide what follow-up, if any, you'll do, and write the date in a note column so next month you remember what you said.

3. Enter last month's costs (10 minutes). Go through card and bank transactions plus any paper receipts. One row per cost, with a unit and a category. Write where the receipt lives ("Drive/Receipts/2026", "email from plumber"). That one column saves hours at year end.

4. Check what's coming (3 minutes). Which leases end in the next 90 days? Is any notice date close? Which recurring jobs are due in the next 30 days, and are any repairs past their follow-up date?

5. Glance at the totals (2 minutes). Rent collected against expected, and income minus expenses per unit so far this year. You're not judging yet, just checking nothing looks odd, like a unit with zero income that isn't vacant.

That's the whole routine. It works because it's the same every month, and because the bank statement keeps the sheet honest.

The year-end check

Once a year, before you talk to an accountant or do your own return:

  • Make sure all 12 months are closed (step 1 done for each).
  • Total rent received and expenses by category, per unit and overall.
  • List any money still owed by tenants and every deposit still held.
  • Confirm every expense row has a receipt location.
  • Start a fresh copy for the new year, carrying over open balances, deposits and active leases.

Your categories should stay generic. Whether and how a cost counts for tax depends on where you live, so map them with your accountant rather than guessing.

Seven mistakes that quietly break a rental spreadsheet

  1. A month-by-month grid of "paid" ticks. It has nowhere to put a half payment. Use one row per rent period due and one per payment.
  2. Typing over formulas. Colour the cells you type in and leave the rest alone.
  3. Free-typed units and categories. "U1", "U01" and "Unit 1" become three different units in your totals. Use dropdowns.
  4. Mixing currencies in one file. Keep one currency per file.
  5. Deposits treated as income. A deposit is money you may have to give back. Track it separately from rent.
  6. No receipt location. An expense you can't back up is hard to explain later.
  7. Updating only when something goes wrong. By then you're reconstructing months from memory. The monthly close prevents that.
